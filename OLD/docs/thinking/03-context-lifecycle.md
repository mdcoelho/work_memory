# Context Lifecycle

## Born

A context is born when scattered facts become one meaningful situation.

It can start from a question, a problem, a meeting, a supplier message, a customer need, a product issue, a production change, or a decision that now requires follow-up.

The birth moment is often small. The founder notices that something needs attention and starts holding a mental thread.

## Named

A context often receives a temporary name before it is understood.

The first name can be wrong, vague, or based on the visible artifact. That does not matter. The name is only a handle for the underlying situation.

Names change as understanding improves.

## Expanded

A context expands when more evidence attaches to it.

A simple supplier question can grow to include product constraints, design state, BOM implications, cost, timing, customer impact, and management reporting.

Expansion increases mental load because the founder must remember more relationships.

## Split

A context splits when one situation reveals multiple independent threads.

Examples:

- A supplier issue reveals one commercial question and one product-design question.
- A product problem reveals one urgent customer answer and one longer industrialization issue.
- A meeting decision creates separate follow-ups for production, supplier communication, and reporting.

Splitting is expensive because the founder must preserve the parent context and the new child contexts.

## Merge

Contexts merge when two separate threads turn out to be the same underlying situation.

A supplier delay, a BOM mismatch, and a drawing question can become one context when the founder realizes they share the same cause.

Merging is valuable because it reduces duplicate thinking.

## Pause

A context pauses when the founder stops working on it but it remains alive.

The pause point is fragile. It includes what was understood, what was uncertain, what changed recently, and what should happen next.

If the pause point is not preserved, resuming later becomes reconstruction.

## Resume

A context resumes when the founder returns and can recover the mental thread.

Good resumption does not require remembering the whole history. It requires enough continuity to understand the current state, recent change, active decision, and next meaningful step.

## Evolve

Contexts evolve over days or weeks.

What began as a problem can become a decision, then a supplier follow-up, then a production implication, then a reporting item.

The identity remains because the underlying situation remains connected, even when the name, artifacts, and people involved change.

## Disappear

A context disappears when it no longer requires memory.

It can disappear because the situation is resolved, abandoned, merged into another context, or no longer relevant.

Disappearance should be intentional enough that the founder is not forced to wonder whether the context was finished or only forgotten.

## Identity over name

The most important lifecycle rule is that context identity is deeper than names.

If the founder returns after three weeks, the product should preserve the thread even if the labels used three weeks ago no longer match how the work is described today.
