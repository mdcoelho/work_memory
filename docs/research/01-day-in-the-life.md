# Day in the Life

## Research status

This document is based only on existing repository documentation.

No user interviews, diary studies, analytics, support tickets, screen recordings, or field observations exist in the repository. Treat this as a research hypothesis map, not validated evidence.

## Working day hypothesis

The target user appears to be a knowledge worker whose day is shaped by context switching.

The repository describes a person who moves between meetings, messages, documents, decisions, urgent requests, operational questions, unfinished thoughts, and partially completed work. Their day does not fail because they forget every task. It fails because they lose the context around what already happened.

## Possible day pattern

### Starting the day

The user may begin with obligations already spread across calendars, messages, task lists, documents, and memory.

The likely tension is not "What should I do today?" alone. It is "What was already happening, and what context do I need before I touch the next thing?"

### Entering work

The user starts a thread of work with context in their head:

- What they were trying to do.
- Why it mattered.
- What had already changed.
- What decisions were still open.
- What state the work was in.

The repository assumes this context is fragile.

### Being interrupted

The user is pulled away by a meeting, message, request, or another responsibility.

The interruption is not only a time cost. It breaks the chain between the work, the reasoning, and the user's short-term memory.

### Switching context

The user moves into another workstream before the previous one has been fully externalized.

The repository assumes this creates a memory gap. The user may keep the task itself but lose the surrounding context: what changed, what they tried, why they paused, and where to resume.

### Returning later

The user comes back and reconstructs context from scattered sources:

- Chat.
- Calendar.
- Documents.
- Files.
- Task lists.
- Notes.
- Memory.

This reconstruction is described as slow, incomplete, and mentally expensive.

### End of day

The user may know they were busy or productive but struggle to say what actually happened.

The repository repeatedly distinguishes future obligations from remembered work. The day ends with a recall problem: "What did I actually do?"

## Pains visible in the day

- Context disappears during interruptions.
- Work is scattered across multiple tools.
- The user remembers obligations better than completed activity.
- Decisions and reasoning are hard to reconstruct later.
- Reporting progress takes effort because evidence is fragmented.
- Handoffs are weak when context was never captured.
- Timers may show duration but not meaning.
- Tools that require structure before capture may be too heavy for real interruptions.

## Assumptions and research questions

| Assumption | Questions to answer before product decisions |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge workers lose context often enough to create a product opportunity. | How many context losses happen in a normal day? Which losses matter? Which are harmless? |
| Interruptions are the main cause of lost context. | What actually causes context loss: interruptions, tool switching, meetings, fatigue, unclear ownership, or poor habits? |
| Users remember future tasks better than completed work. | Can users accurately recall completed work at the end of the day? What do they forget first? |
| Reconstructing work is painful. | How often do users reconstruct their day? What triggers reconstruction? How long does it take? |
| The problem is personal before it is collaborative. | Do users want private memory first, or do they mainly need shared visibility? |
| Users will capture during interruption. | What would make them stop long enough to capture? What moments are too urgent for capture? |
| A chronological record maps to how users recall work. | Do users think in time, workstreams, people, artifacts, outcomes, or decisions? |
| The end-of-day recall problem is important. | Is the highest-value moment end-of-day review, in-the-moment resume, weekly reporting, handoff, or something else? |

## Unknowns

- The actual job role with the strongest pain is unknown.
- The frequency of context loss is unknown.
- The severity of context loss is unknown.
- The user's current coping mechanisms are unknown.
- The emotional weight of the problem is unknown.
- The difference between solo work and team work is unknown.
- The moment when users are most willing to capture is unknown.

## Contradictions to investigate

- The documentation says users should capture in seconds, but also says users are constantly interrupted. It is unknown whether users have enough attention during interruptions to capture anything.
- The documentation values Work Logs over timers, but it is unknown whether users currently rely on time data for reporting, billing, accountability, or self-awareness.
- The documentation rejects structure before capture, but recovery may require some structure. The right amount is unknown.

## Research direction

The next research activity should observe real workdays, not ask users abstractly whether they lose context.

Useful methods:

- Diary study across several workdays.
- End-of-day recall exercise.
- Context-switch interview using a real recent interruption.
- Tool-reconstruction walkthrough where the user shows how they recover what happened.
